This tool writes to data brokers on your behalf and tracks what comes back. Which law you are exercising - and, more importantly, which companies are worth writing to first - depends on where you live. Read the section that applies to you.

If you are in the EU or the UK

GDPR Article 17 · UK GDPR

Your position is the strong one. Erasure is a right, not a favour: the request is free, the company has one month to answer, and "we would rather not" is not an answer. Every request this tool sends on your behalf cites that right explicitly.

The catch is that most of these companies probably hold no file with your name on it. What circulates about you is mostly hashed - your email address run through a one-way function, then matched against advertising IDs, device graphs and household clusters. Nobody in that chain needs to know you are you; they only need the same hash to keep turning up.

That is why a letter to every marketing company in the list is not the best first move. The hashes are only useful because a small number of companies do the joining. Start with them - the list below - and you cut off far more than you would by writing to a hundred companies that never knew your name.

If you are in the United States

State privacy laws · CCPA/CPRA · FCRA

There is no single federal right to erasure. What you have instead is a growing patchwork of state laws - California, Colorado, Connecticut, Virginia, Texas, Oregon and more each year - most of which give residents the right to have personal data deleted and to opt out of its sale or sharing. California residents have the strongest version, and its registry of data brokers is the reason so many of the companies in this tool are listed at all.

The difference from Europe is that here these companies very likely do hold a file on you by name: address history, household composition, income bracket, purchase behaviour, inferred interests. The people-search sites that publish your address are the most visible end of it, and the ones most worth clearing first.

Two things a deletion request will not do: it will not erase your credit file at Experian, Equifax or TransUnion (that is governed separately by the FCRA - you can opt out of prescreened offers, but the file itself stays), and it will not stop the hash-matching described on the left. For that, the same list below applies to you too.

Where the law currently stands

Consent strings are personal data

In 2022 the Belgian data protection authority found IAB Europe's Transparency and Consent Framework - the consent pop-up standard behind most of the ad industry - in breach of the GDPR, holding that the "TC String" recording your choices is itself personal data and that IAB Europe acts as a joint controller for it. An action plan was approved in 2023; appeals have run alongside it. The practical point for you: consent captured through those banners is contested ground, not settled permission.

"Legitimate interest" is allowed, but not unlimited

In February 2023 a UK tribunal largely overturned the ICO's enforcement notice against Experian, accepting that legitimate interest can be a lawful basis for direct marketing. It also rejected Experian's argument that telling millions of people their data was being processed was too expensive to be proportionate - cost is not a defence against the duty to inform. Both halves matter when a company answers your request by citing legitimate interest.

What to do next

  1. Set up your profile and mailbox in Settings, so requests go out as you and replies can be read back.
  2. Open Brokers and start with the companies above, then work down by priority.
